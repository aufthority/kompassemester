The mole · C1

A mole is a counting unit.

Luna's caseLuna's first thought: the two chemicals don't react gram for gram. To compare them, she has to count their particles.

We already count in groups: a dozen is 12 eggs, a ream is 500 sheets. Chemists count atoms and molecules in a much bigger group, the mole (symbol mol).

6.022 × 10²³
particles in one mole. This is the Avogadro constant, NA, in mol⁻¹.
Any particle
A mole can be of atoms, molecules, ions or formula units. Always say which.
The mole concept: one mole of any substance contains 6.022 × 10²³ of its particles, and its mass in grams equals its relative formula mass. So weighing a sample tells you how many particles it holds.

Until 2019 the mole was defined as the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the Avogadro constant is fixed at exactly 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, and the mole is defined from it. For calculations, 6.022 × 10²³ is enough. (BIPM, 2019)

The story behind the number

Avogadro never knew his number.

Luna assumes Avogadro measured 6.022 × 10²³. He didn't. The idea and the number came from a relay of chemists across nearly a century:

1811Avogadro proposes that equal volumes of gases hold equal numbers of particles. Almost nobody listens.
1860Cannizzaro revives the idea at the Karlsruhe Congress and uses it to fix atomic masses.
1865Loschmidt makes the first estimate of how many particles are in a volume of gas.
1894Ostwald coins the word Mol, from the German Molekül.
1909Perrin measures the number, names it after Avogadro, and wins the 1926 Nobel Prize for the work.

The constant honours Avogadro's idea, not his measurement. Avogadro constant; Mole (unit)

The mole · C1

From the periodic table to grams per mole.

Each element has a relative atomic mass (Ar), listed on the periodic table. Add up the Ar of every atom in a formula and you get the relative formula mass (Mr). Put "grams per mole" after it and you have the molar mass, M.

ElementArElementArElementAr
H1O16Cl35.5
C12Na23K39
N14Mg24Ca40

These rounded values are used throughout this explainer and the lab. Others used: Al 27, S 32, Ba 137.

Molar mass is a conversion factor: water has M = 18 g/mol, so 18 g of water is 1 mol, and 9 g is 0.5 mol.
Worked examples

Add the atoms, count every one.

Multiply each Ar by the number of that atom in the formula, then add. Brackets multiply everything inside them. Tap each card to check the working.

Ionic compounds such as NaCl have no molecules, so their particles are called formula units. The arithmetic is the same: 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g/mol.

Key relationships

Moles sit in the middle.

Every calculation in this topic passes through moles. Divide a mass by the molar mass to get moles; multiply moles by the Avogadro constant to get particles.

Paracetamol tablet: n = 0.500 g ÷ 151 g/mol = 3.31 × 10⁻³ mol, and N = 3.31 × 10⁻³ × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 2.0 × 10²¹ molecules.

A handy shortcut: milligrams ÷ molar mass gives millimoles (mmol). 500 mg ÷ 151 g/mol = 3.31 mmol. Pharmacy uses mmol constantly.

In the pharmacy

Some labels speak in millimoles.

Luna's case · Day 1Between tasks, Luna is asked to check a carton of potassium chloride tablets made at the factory. Is the second number on the label right?

Potassium chloride tablets for low blood potassium are sold as "600 mg (8 mmol)". The second number is the mole concept at work. (NHS dm+d)

n(KCl) = 600 mg ÷ 74.5 g/mol = 8.05 mmolone K⁺ ion per KCl, so each tablet supplies about 8 mmol of potassium

Doctors prescribe electrolytes by the amount of ion the patient needs, not by the mass of salt. Two salts of the same ion can have very different masses per mmol.

The body responds to the number of potassium ions, not the mass of the salt they came in. 600 mg of potassium chloride and 600 mg of potassium citrate give different amounts of K⁺, because the two salts have different molar masses. Millimoles compare them fairly.

Check your understanding

Three quick mole questions.

QUESTION 1 OF 3
How many moles are in 9 g of water (M = 18 g/mol)?
n = m ÷ M = 9 ÷ 18 = 0.5 mol. Multiplying (9 × 18) is the most common slip.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
Which contains more particles: 1 mol of NaCl or 1 mol of paracetamol?
One mole of anything contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles. The masses differ (58.5 g vs 151 g); the counts do not.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
A dose of 250 mg of a drug with M = 250 g/mol is:
mg ÷ g/mol gives mmol: 250 ÷ 250 = 1 mmol, which is 0.001 mol.
Types of reaction · C1

Bonds break, new substances form.

Luna's case · Day 2While the trial batch is analysed, Luna helps in the quality control (QC) lab. Every test there is a chemical reaction, and each one belongs to a type.

In a chemical reaction, the reactants are turned into products with different properties. The atoms are not destroyed; they are rearranged into new combinations.

You can often tell a reaction has happened from what you see:

Gas bubbles appear A solid (precipitate) forms in a solution The colour changes Heat is given out or taken in Light is given out
Dissolving sugar or melting ice is a physical change: no new substance forms, and it can be reversed easily.
Types of reaction · C1

Six patterns cover most reactions.

Reactions are grouped by what happens to the reactants. Letters stand for elements or groups of atoms.

TypePattern
Combination (synthesis)A + B → AB
DecompositionAB → A + B
Single displacementA + BC → AC + B
Double displacementAB + CD → AD + CB
Neutralisationacid + base → salt + water
Combustionfuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (for compounds of C and H)

Neutralisation is a special kind of double displacement, but it is important enough to have its own name.

Types of reaction · C1

Redox is a lens, not a seventh box.

A redox reaction is one where electrons move from one substance to another. Oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain ("OIL RIG"). Both always happen together.

Redox

electrons transferred
Most combination, decomposition and single displacement reactions, and all combustion.

Not redox

ions swap partners
Neutralisation and precipitation (double displacement).

Example

Zn + Cu²⁺
Zn loses 2 electrons (oxidised); Cu²⁺ gains them (reduced).

Give each atom an oxidation number: elements are 0, O is usually −2, H is usually +1, and the numbers in a neutral compound add to zero. If an atom's number rises, it was oxidised; if it falls, it was reduced. In 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, Mg goes 0 → +2 (oxidised) and O goes 0 → −2 (reduced).

The two compounds swap partners and insoluble BaSO₄ precipitates: double displacement. No acid or base, so not neutralisation. This reaction is the basis of the limit test for sulfate.
Equations · C2

No atom is created or destroyed.

The law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants, because the same atoms are only rearranged.

An equation must show this. A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides of the arrow.

Luna's case · Day 3Luna writes up the batch record. If no atom is created or destroyed, her missing 5.30 g cannot simply have vanished.
The numbers in front of each formula, the coefficients, are what every stoichiometry calculation uses.
Equations · C2

Words first, then formulas.

Start with a word equation, swap each name for its correct formula, then balance, then add state symbols.

Word equationmagnesium hydroxide + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + water
FormulasMg(OH)₂ + HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O
BalancedMg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
Mg(OH)₂(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

(s) solid · (l) liquid · (g) gas · (aq) dissolved in water

Charges must cancel. Na⁺, K⁺, Ag⁺, NH₄⁺ (1+); Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺ (2+); Al³⁺ (3+); Cl⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, HCO₃⁻ (1−); O²⁻, SO₄²⁻, CO₃²⁻ (2−). So Mg²⁺ with OH⁻ needs two hydroxides: Mg(OH)₂. Al³⁺ with SO₄²⁻ gives Al₂(SO₄)₃.

Equations · C2

Change coefficients, never subscripts.

Hydrogen burns to give water. The skeleton equation has 2 oxygen atoms on the left and 1 on the right:

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O
Wrong: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂. The atoms now balance, but H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. Changing a subscript changes the chemical.
Right: put a 2 in front of H₂O to fix oxygen, then a 2 in front of H₂ to fix hydrogen.
2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

A coefficient multiplies the whole formula: 2H₂O means 4 H atoms and 2 O atoms.

Equations · C2

Balance in a fixed order.

  • 1
    Write correct formulas for every reactant and product.
  • 2
    Balance metals first, then non-metals other than H and O.
  • 3
    Treat a polyatomic ion that appears unchanged on both sides (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻) as one unit.
  • 4
    Balance hydrogen, then oxygen last.
  • 5
    Reduce to the smallest whole numbers, then recount every atom.

Propane burning: C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O. Carbon: 3CO₂. Hydrogen: 4H₂O. Oxygen on the right is now 6 + 4 = 10, so 5O₂.

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Key concept

Coefficients are mole ratios.

A balanced equation can be read at three levels. The coefficients count particles, so they also count moles.

Mg(OH)₂+ 2HCl→ MgCl₂+ 2H₂O
Particles1212
Moles1 mol2 mol1 mol2 mol
Mass58 g73 g95 g36 g

Check the mass row: 58 + 73 = 131 g on the left and 95 + 36 = 131 g on the right. Mass is conserved.

Masses are not in the ratio of the coefficients. 1 mol of Mg(OH)₂ reacts with 2 mol of HCl, but 58 g reacts with 73 g, not with 116 g. Always convert to moles before using the ratio.

Three questions on equations.

QUESTION 1 OF 3
Which coefficients balance __Al + __O₂ → __Al₂O₃ ?
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃: 4 Al and 6 O on each side. "2, 3, 1" gives 6 O on the left but only 3 on the right.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
Why must you not balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O by writing H₂O₂?
H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide. Subscripts define the substance; only coefficients may change.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O. How many moles of HCl react with 0.10 mol of Mg(OH)₂?
The ratio is 1 : 2, so 0.10 × 2 = 0.20 mol of HCl.
Stoichiometry · C3

Grams of one substance to grams of another.

Luna's case · Day 4To track the missing mass, Luna has to turn grams of one substance into grams of another.

Stoichiometry is the calculation of how much of each substance reacts or forms, using a balanced equation. Every problem follows the same road, and the mole ratio is the only bridge between two substances.

Mass of Ag
Moles of A÷ M of A
Moles of B× mole ratio B : A
Mass of B× M of B
You cannot jump from grams of A straight to grams of B. Different substances have different molar masses, so equal masses are not equal amounts.
Worked example

How much acid does one tablet neutralise?

An antacid tablet contains 500 mg of Mg(OH)₂. How much HCl can it neutralise?

Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
  • 1
    Moles of Mg(OH)₂ = 500 mg ÷ 58 g/mol = 8.62 mmol
  • 2
    Mole ratio HCl : Mg(OH)₂ = 2 : 1, so HCl = 2 × 8.62 = 17.2 mmol
  • 3
    Mass of HCl = 17.2 mmol × 36.5 g/mol = 629 mg

Antacid strength is reported as acid-neutralising capacity in milliequivalents (mEq). For HCl, 1 mEq is 1 mmol, so this tablet could in theory neutralise 17.2 mEq. US rules require an antacid product to provide at least 5 mEq, measured by the USP test method. (21 CFR 331.10)

Try it

Your turn: calcium carbonate.

A 1.00 g calcium carbonate tablet (M = 100 g/mol) reacts completely with stomach acid.

CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂
QUESTION 1 OF 2
How many millimoles of HCl does it neutralise?
1.00 g ÷ 100 g/mol = 10 mmol CaCO₃. The ratio is 1 : 2, so 20 mmol HCl.
QUESTION 2 OF 2
What mass of carbon dioxide (M = 44 g/mol) forms? This gas is why calcium carbonate can cause burping.
The ratio CO₂ : CaCO₃ is 1 : 1, so 10 mmol CO₂ × 44 g/mol = 440 mg = 0.44 g.
Limiting reagent · C3

Whatever runs out first sets the limit.

Reactants are rarely mixed in exactly the ratio of the equation. The limiting reagent is the reactant that is used up first; it decides how much product can form. Everything else is in excess and some is left over.

With 10 slices of bread and 3 slices of cheese, you can make only 3 sandwiches, even though you have more bread. Cheese is limiting; 4 slices of bread are in excess.

The limiting reagent is not simply the one with the smallest mass. You must compare moles, scaled by the coefficients.
Limiting reagent · C3

Four steps to find it.

  • 1
    Write the balanced equation.
  • 2
    Convert the mass of each reactant to moles.
  • 3
    Divide each number of moles by that reactant's coefficient. The smallest result marks the limiting reagent.
  • 4
    Calculate the product from the limiting reagent only. The excess reagent left over = moles present − moles used.
Step 3 is the "how many sandwiches can each ingredient make?" question: bread 10 ÷ 2 = 5, cheese 3 ÷ 1 = 3. The smaller answer, 3, wins.

Luna's case · worked example

Luna's batch: which runs out?

The trial batch reacted 2.00 g of salicylic acid with 5.40 g of acetic anhydride (5.0 mL; density 1.08 g/mL).

C₇H₆O₃ + C₄H₆O₃ → C₉H₈O₄ + C₂H₄O₂salicylic acid (138) + acetic anhydride (102) → aspirin (180) + acetic acid (60)
Moles÷ coefficient
Salicylic acid2.00 ÷ 138 = 0.0145 mol0.0145 → limiting
Acetic anhydride5.40 ÷ 102 = 0.0529 mol0.0529 → excess

Ratio aspirin : salicylic acid is 1 : 1, so the most aspirin possible is 0.0145 mol × 180 g/mol = 2.61 g. Acetic anhydride left over: 0.0529 − 0.0145 = 0.0384 mol, which is 3.92 g.

Luna's case · ClueMost of the "missing" mass was never going to become aspirin: 3.92 g of acetic anhydride was in excess and never reacted.

Check your understanding

Three questions on limiting reagents.

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
QUESTION 1 OF 3
You mix 4 mol of H₂ with 3 mol of O₂. Which is limiting?
H₂: 4 ÷ 2 = 2. O₂: 3 ÷ 1 = 3. H₂ gives the smaller number, so it runs out first; 1 mol of O₂ is left.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
How many moles of water form from that mixture?
Work from the limiting reagent: H₂O : H₂ is 2 : 2, so 4 mol H₂ gives 4 mol H₂O. Using O₂ (6 mol) would overestimate.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
Why did Luna's factory make salicylic acid the limiting reagent?
The more valuable material is made limiting so that as much of it as possible ends up as product. Mass alone never decides it.
Percentage yield · C3

What you could make vs what you made.

The theoretical yield is the maximum mass of product, calculated from the limiting reagent. The actual yield is the mass you weigh at the end.

Percentage yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100%

Luna's batch gave 2.10 g of dry aspirin, so its percentage yield is 2.10 ÷ 2.61 × 100 = 80.5%.

Percentage yield · C3

Real reactions lose product.

Actual yield is almost always below 100%. Tap each card to see why.

Over 100%? That is a warning sign, not a success. The product is probably still wet or contains impurities. Dry it to constant mass and check purity.
Luna's case · solved

Case closed: nothing went missing.

Mass is conserved, so Luna accounts for every gram:

1
3.92 g of acetic anhydride was in excess and never reacted.
2
0.87 g became acetic acid, the by-product the balanced equation predicts.
3
The limiting reagent allowed at most 2.61 g of aspirin. Luna collected 2.10 g (80.5%); 0.51 g was lost during purification.
Luna's case · Her report"The batch worked. To raise the yield, cut purification losses. Adding more acetic anhydride won't help, because salicylic acid is the limiting reagent."
Try it

Check a percentage yield.

Percentage yield shows how well you carried out the reaction. Atom economy shows how much of the reactants' mass can ever end up in the desired product, even at 100% yield:

atom economy = M(desired product) ÷ total M of reactants × 100%. For aspirin: 180 ÷ (138 + 102) × 100 = 75%. The other 25% leaves as acetic acid. Green chemistry favours routes with high atom economy and high yield.

Check your understanding

Two questions on yield.

QUESTION 1 OF 2
The theoretical yield is 5.0 g and you collect 4.2 g. What is the percentage yield?
4.2 ÷ 5.0 × 100 = 84%. Dividing the wrong way round gives 119%.
QUESTION 2 OF 2
A student reports a 108% yield. What is the most likely explanation?
Mass cannot appear from nowhere. Extra mass usually means solvent or impurities are still in the product.
