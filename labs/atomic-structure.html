Specimen 01// hydrogen, shells

One proton. One electron. Done.

Hydrogen is the simplest atom that exists: a single electron in the lowest energy level available, the 1s orbital.

Specimen 01// hydrogen, nucleus

No neutrons at all.

The most common hydrogen isotope, protium, has a nucleus that is just one proton — no neutrons. It's the only stable atom built this way.

Specimen 02// neon, shells

Both shells, completely full.

Neon's ten electrons fill the first shell (2) and the second shell (8) exactly. Nothing left over, nowhere to put another. That's a noble gas: it doesn't react.

Specimen 02// neon, nucleus

Ten and ten, balanced.

Neon-20, the common isotope, packs ten protons and ten neutrons together — held by the strong force at a range far shorter than the electron shells above it.

Specimen 03// sodium, shells

One electron, all alone, in a new shell.

Sodium is neon plus one more electron — and that electron can't fit in the second shell. It opens a third shell, and sits there, shielded from the nucleus by the ten inner electrons, so it is easy to pull away. That lone electron is why sodium is so reactive.

Specimen 03// sodium, nucleus

More neutrons than protons.

Sodium-23's nucleus needs one more neutron than proton to stay stable — heavier nuclei generally need this extra neutron "glue" to hold together against proton-proton repulsion.

The sheet, complete

Same rules. Different outcomes.

One electron makes hydrogen reactive by being alone. Ten electrons make neon inert by being complete. The difference between a noble gas and a reactive metal is a single electron's address.
