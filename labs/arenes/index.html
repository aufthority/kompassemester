Three painkillers, one ring.

Aspirin, paracetamol and ibuprofen are on every pharmacy shelf. Each one is built on the same six-carbon ring: benzene.

Your guess: what happens to that ring inside the body, or in a reaction flask?

Compounds with a benzene ring are called arenes (aromatic hydrocarbons and their derivatives). Use Next to move through the pages. A periodic table is in Contents.

From coal and oil to the pharmacy.

Meet benzene · watch

C₆H₆: six carbons, six equal bonds.

Benzene is a flat ring of six carbons, each with one H. Every carbon is sp², and every bond angle is 120°.

Kekulé drew it with three C=C double bonds. But all six C–C bonds are the same length (1.39 Å), between a single bond (1.54 Å) and a double bond (1.34 Å).

So the real molecule is neither Kekulé drawing. We draw either one, or a circle inside the ring. (Bruice 7.1, 15.1)

Meet benzene · tap each card

Six electrons, shared by six carbons.

Sharing electrons over more atoms lowers their energy. That is where benzene’s extra stability comes from. (Bruice 7.6, 15.1)

Naming · tap each card

Six common names you must use.

These groups give the whole compound a special name. Learn them like words.

Not “hydroxybenzene” or “aminobenzene”: the common names are the accepted ones. (Unit 5 notes; Bruice 15.7)

Naming · open each one

One group: group name + benzene.

Other single groups are named as a prefix in front of benzene. One group needs no number.

bromobenzene · chlorobenzene · nitrobenzene · ethylbenzene

Compare the chain with the ring. 6 carbons or fewer: benzene is the parent, e.g. propylbenzene. 7 or more: the chain is the parent and the ring becomes a phenyl group (C₆H₅–), e.g. 2-phenylheptane: the ring sits on C2 of a 7-carbon chain.

Phenyl is the ring itself, C₆H₅–. Benzyl is the ring plus one CH₂, C₆H₅CH₂–, as in benzyl alcohol (C₆H₅CH₂OH), a preservative in injections.

(Unit 5 notes; Bruice 15.7)

Naming · tap the ring

Two groups: ortho, meta, para.

With two groups, say where the second sits compared with the first:

ortho (o) = 1,2 · meta (m) = 1,3 · para (p) = 1,4

Tap a ring carbon to place a second Br. Find ortho, meta and para.

Naming · open each example

Numbers: as low as possible.

  1. If a group has a common name (toluene, phenol, aniline, benzoic acid …), its carbon is C1 and the name ends with that parent.
  2. Number round the ring to give the lowest set of numbers.
  3. List the groups in alphabetical order (ignore di-, tri-). If the numbers tie, the group first in the alphabet gets the lower number.

Both ways give 1,3. Tie: bromo comes before chloro, so Br gets 1: 1-bromo-3-chlorobenzene (or m-bromochlorobenzene).

OH makes it a phenol, so OH is C1. The Br atoms sit on 2, 4 and 6: 2,4,6-tribromophenol.

No common-name group. Try every start: the lowest set is 1,2,4. Alphabetical: 2-bromo-4-chloro-1-nitrobenzene. “1-nitro-2-bromo-4-chloro…” has the right numbers but the wrong order.

(Unit 5 notes; Bruice 16.1)

Aromaticity and stability · open it

Benzene keeps 152 kJ/mol back.

Adding H₂ to one C=C (cyclohexene → cyclohexane) releases 120 kJ/mol (28.6 kcal/mol). If benzene had three ordinary C=C bonds, it should release 3 × 120 = 360 kJ/mol.

Benzene really releases only 208 kJ/mol (49.8 kcal/mol). It started lower in energy.

Extra stability = expected − measured = 360 − 208 = 152 kJ/mol (about 36 kcal/mol). This is benzene’s aromatic stabilisation, from its delocalised π electrons.

Compare cyclohexa-1,3-diene: expected 2 × 120 = 240, measured 232, so only 8 kJ/mol extra. Conjugation helps a little; aromaticity helps a lot.

(Bruice 7.6; Unit 5 notes)

Aromaticity · tap each test

Aromatic? Pass all four tests.

Tests 1 to 3 together give an unbroken ring of p orbitals (a cyclic π cloud). Test 4 counts the electrons in it. (Bruice 15.1)

Aromaticity · tap each card

Count only π electrons in the ring.

A group outside the ring (the CH₃ of toluene) adds nothing to the count. (Bruice 15.2)

Aromaticity · try it

Five rings: aromatic or not?

Judge all five rings correctly to continue.

Pyridine (C₅H₅N) is benzene with one CH swapped for N. N is sp²: its p orbital joins the cloud with 1 electron, and its lone pair sits in the plane of the ring, outside the cloud. So: 6 π electrons, aromatic. Many medicines contain it (for example isoniazid). (Bruice 15.3)

QUESTION 1 OF 3
Cyclohexa-1,3-diene is expected to release 240 kJ/mol on hydrogenation and really releases 232. Its extra stability is:
240 − 232 = 8 kJ/mol: conjugation only, not aromatic.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
Cyclobutadiene is flat, cyclic and every carbon has a p orbital. Why is it not aromatic?
2 C=C × 2 = 4. Hückel numbers are 2, 6, 10: 4 fails.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
How many π electrons does the cyclopentadienyl anion have?
2 C=C (4) + lone pair (2) = 6: aromatic.
How benzene reacts · try it

Bromine water: add or substitute?

An alkene adds Br₂ across its C=C at once, so orange bromine water turns colourless. Benzene has three “C=C” in its drawing. Does it do the same?

Do both steps.

How benzene reacts · open each one

Benzene keeps its ring.

In electrophilic aromatic substitution, an electrophile (E⁺, an electron-poor particle) replaces one H on the ring:

C₆H₆ + E⁺ → C₆H₅E + H⁺

Addition would leave a ring with only two C=C: the 152 kJ/mol of aromatic stability would be lost. Substitution gives back an aromatic ring, so it wins.

Benzene’s π electrons are held more tightly than an alkene’s, so it reacts only with a strong electrophile. The catalyst (FeBr₃, AlCl₃, H₂SO₄) makes that strong electrophile.

(Bruice 15.8)

How benzene reacts · watch

Step 1 slow, step 2 fast.

Step 1 (slow): two π electrons of the ring attack the electrophile. A new C–E bond forms, and the ring becomes a carbocation. It is no longer aromatic.

Step 2 (fast): a base removes H⁺ from the same carbon that gained E. Its electrons go back into the ring, which is aromatic again.

All five reactions on the next page follow these two steps. Only the electrophile changes. (Bruice 15.9)

How benzene reacts · tap each card

Five reactions, one pattern.

The by-product is always the H⁺ from the ring joined to what is left of the reagent. (Bruice 15.10–15.14)

How benzene reacts · open each one

The catalyst makes E⁺.

FeBr₃ is a Lewis acid: it accepts a lone pair from one Br. That pulls the Br–Br bond apart, so the other Br acts as Br⁺. FeBr₃ comes back at the end.

Sulfuric acid gives H⁺ to nitric acid. It loses water and leaves the nitronium ion, NO₂⁺.

AlCl₃ takes Cl⁻ from the reagent, leaving a carbocation R⁺ (alkylation) or an acylium ion RCO⁺ (acylation).

A carbocation can rearrange to a more stable one before it attacks. 1-chloropropane with AlCl₃ gives mostly isopropylbenzene, not propylbenzene. Acylation has no such problem. (Bruice 15.14)

(Bruice 15.10–15.14)

How benzene reacts · optional

A second group: ortho/para or meta?

When the ring already has a group, that group decides where the next one goes.

Groups that give electrons to the ring: –CH₃, –OH, –NH₂ (and the halogens). The new group goes to 2 and 4. Toluene + Br₂/FeBr₃ gives 2-bromotoluene and 4-bromotoluene.

Groups that pull electrons from the ring: –NO₂, –COOH, –CHO, –SO₃H. The new group goes to 3. Nitrobenzene + Br₂/FeBr₃ gives 1-bromo-3-nitrobenzene.

This page is optional: the Ring Bench always starts from plain benzene. (Bruice 16.3–16.4)

Arenes in medicines · open each one

A safety lesson: rofecoxib.

Celecoxib and rofecoxib were made to block only COX-2, to cause fewer stomach ulcers than older painkillers. Both are built on benzene rings.

On 30 September 2004, Merck withdrew rofecoxib worldwide. A long trial (APPROVe) showed a higher risk of heart attack and stroke after 18 months of use. (FDA, 2004)

Still in use, but its label carries a boxed warning about serious heart and blood-vessel (thrombotic) events, like other NSAIDs. (Celecoxib label, DailyMed)

Problems can appear only after years of wide use. Pharmacists help by checking risk factors, counselling patients and reporting suspected side effects (in Malaysia, to the NPRA).

Take home

Name it, test it, react it.

1
Benzene, C₆H₆: flat, six sp² carbons, six equal bonds, 6 delocalised π electrons. From coal tar and petroleum.
2
Names: toluene, phenol, aniline, benzoic acid, benzaldehyde, styrene; otherwise group + benzene. 7 or more chain carbons: phenylalkane. Lowest numbers, A to Z; o = 1,2, m = 1,3, p = 1,4.
3
Aromatic: cyclic, a p orbital on every ring atom, flat, 4n + 2 π electrons. Benzene is 152 kJ/mol (36 kcal/mol) more stable than three C=C.
4
Benzene substitutes, it does not add: halogenation, nitration, sulfonation, Friedel–Crafts acylation and alkylation. Step 1 slow (carbocation), step 2 fast (loses H⁺).
