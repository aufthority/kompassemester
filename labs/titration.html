Titration & gravimetry

Count it, or weigh it.

Two flasks look identical; one holds twice the chloride. Measure the difference by the reagent it takes to react (titration), or by the mass of solid it becomes (gravimetry).

Basics · 1/2

Every method asks the same four questions.

1 · What reacts?

Analyte + reagent, in a fixed ratio.

2 · Why does it finish?

The reaction runs to completion: K is huge.

3 · How do we see the end?

A visible signal: a colour, a solid, or no more cloudiness.

4 · How does it become a number?

Ratio × amount of reagent used, or mass of solid formed.

Basics · 2/2

Equivalence vs end point.

Equivalence: reagent exactly matches analyte. End point: where we see it. The gap is titration error; a good indicator keeps it under one drop.

01 · Acid–base · 1/2

An acid hands H⁺ to a base. Count the base it takes.

1 · What reacts?

H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. A weak acid works too: HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O.

2 · Why does it finish?

Water barely splits back (K ≈ 10¹⁴ for H⁺ + OH⁻).

3 · How do we see the end?

The indicator is a weak acid. HIn and In⁻ differ in colour; it flips over ~2 pH units.

4 · How does it become a number?

Mole ratio from the equation × C × V of titrant.

Pharmacy: non-aqueous perchloric acid titration is the standard pharmacopoeial assay for many weak-base drugs.

01 · Acid–base · 2/2

Pick the indicator for the equivalence pH.

Watch outNot pH 7. Weak acid + strong base ends above 7, so use phenolphthalein (8.2–10.0), not methyl orange (3.1–4.4).

02 · Precipitation · 1/4

Silver grabs chloride and drops out as a solid. Count the silver.

1 · What reacts?

Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s), 1 : 1.

2 · Why does it finish?

AgCl barely dissolves (Ksp ≈ 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰): almost no Cl⁻ is left.

3 · How do we see the end?

Three ways: Mohr, Volhard, Fajans.

4 · How does it become a number?

mol Cl⁻ = mol Ag⁺ used.

Pharmacy: chloride content of sodium chloride and potassium chloride preparations.

02 · Precipitation · 3/4

Mohr: who precipitates first.

AgCl needs far less Ag⁺ to form than Ag₂CrO₄ does, so chromate waits until the chloride is gone.

02 · Precipitation · 4/4

Fajans: the surface flips.

The dye never reacts. It sticks to the solid once the surface turns positive.

03 · Redox · 1/3

One gives electrons, the other takes them. Count the electrons.

1 · What reacts?

MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O

2 · Why does it finish?

A big potential gap: MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺ +1.51 V vs Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ +0.77 V.

3 · How do we see the end?

Permanganate is its own indicator: purple persists once the analyte runs out. Iodine: starch turns blue-black.

4 · How does it become a number?

Balance electrons first: MnO₄⁻ takes 5, Fe²⁺ gives 1, so 1 : 5.

03 · Redox · 2/3

The titrant is the indicator.

Watch outAcidify permanganate with sulfuric acid, not hydrochloric. It oxidises Cl⁻ to chlorine, uses extra titrant, and the result reads high.

04 · Complexometric · 1/2

EDTA wraps a metal ion like a claw. Count the claws.

1 · What reacts?

M²⁺ + EDTA⁴⁻ → [M–EDTA]²⁻. Always 1 : 1.

2 · Why does it finish?

Six arms grip at once: very stable (log Kf ≈ 10.7 for Ca²⁺).

3 · How do we see the end?

Eriochrome Black T is wine-red holding the metal, blue when free.

4 · How does it become a number?

mol metal = mol EDTA. The simplest ratio here.

Pharmacy: calcium salts (e.g. calcium gluconate), zinc salts, and Al/Mg in antacids.

04 · Complexometric · 2/2

The end point is a steal.

The indicator must hold the metal more weakly than EDTA does, or it never lets go.

Watch outBuffer at pH 10 (ammonia) for Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺. Too acidic: H⁺ blocks EDTA. Too alkaline: the metal precipitates as hydroxide.
05 · Gravimetric · 1/3

Make a pure solid of known formula. Weigh it.

1 · What reacts?

Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s)

2 · Why does it finish?

BaSO₄ barely dissolves (Ksp ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁰); excess Ba²⁺ pushes it further.

3 · How do we see the end?

Add a drop more reagent to the clear liquid. No new cloudiness: complete.

4 · How does it become a number?

Mass of solid × gravimetric factor = mass of analyte.

05 · Gravimetric · 2/3

From cloud to crucible.

A good precipitate is very insoluble, pure, of known formula, with crystals big enough to filter.

Watch outWeigh only at constant mass. A wet precipitate weighs too much and the result reads high.

Pharmacy: loss on drying, sulfated ash and residue on ignition are gravimetric tests.

05 · Gravimetric · 3/3

What goes wrong: occlusion.

A crystal that grows too fast traps stray ions. Washing can't reach them, so the mass reads high. Slow, hot, dilute precipitation and digestion prevent it.

Summary

Five methods, one frame.

About the numbers. Ksp, E°, Kf, indicator ranges and the Mohr pH window are standard reference values recalled without lookup; treat them as approximate until checked (CRC Handbook; Harris, Quantitative Chemical Analysis). Pharmacy examples name general uses only, not monograph conditions. Particles are representative, not to scale.

Primary standard: weighed straight into solution. High purity, stable, not hygroscopic, high molar mass (potassium hydrogen phthalate, sodium carbonate, sodium chloride). A secondary standard, such as NaOH, is first standardised against a primary one.

Direct vs back titration. Direct: titrant reacts with the analyte itself. Back: add a known excess of reagent, then titrate what is left. Useful when the reaction is slow or the end point is poor.

Blank titration. Run the procedure without sample and subtract its titre, to cancel reagent impurities and the indicator's own consumption.

For a reaction a A + b T → products (A = analyte, T = titrant):

nA = ab × CT × VT

Titration error = Vend point − Vequivalence. A good indicator keeps it under one drop (~0.05 mL).

Very weak bases (many amine drugs) barely react in water, because water competes as a base and blurs the end point.

Dissolve them in glacial acetic acid and titrate with perchloric acid in acetic acid. Acetic acid is a poorer base than water, so the drug's basicity stands out and the end point sharpens.

End point by crystal violet (violet → blue-green) or potentiometrically. Keep reagents dry: water blunts the end point.

pH = pKIn + log [In⁻][HIn]

The eye sees a change as the ratio moves from about 1:10 to 10:1, so the range is roughly pKIn ± 1. Equivalence pH for a weak acid titrated with strong base:

pH = 7 + ½ pKa + ½ log Csalt

Only fully deprotonated EDTA (Y⁴⁻) binds. Kf is scaled by the fraction present as Y⁴⁻ at the working pH:

Kf′ = αY⁴⁻ × Kf

αY⁴⁻ falls steeply below pH 8, which is why the buffer matters.
