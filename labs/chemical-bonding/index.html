Start here

Lighter, yet it boils 160 degrees higher.

Water and hydrogen sulfide look like twins: each is one central atom from group 16 with two hydrogens. Hydrogen sulfide is almost twice as heavy, so you might expect it to boil at the higher temperature. It does the opposite. Water boils at 100 °C; hydrogen sulfide boils at about −60 °C. (PubChem)

If mass alone decided, water would be a gas in your dispensary, and there would be no injections, syrups or eye drops made with it. The difference comes from how the atoms are bonded, what shape the molecule takes, and how the molecules attract each other. That is this explainer.

2 · Why bonds form

A bond forms because it lowers the energy.

As two atoms approach, each nucleus starts to attract the other atom's electrons. The energy of the pair falls. Push them too close, though, and the two positive nuclei repel, so the energy shoots back up.

The bond sits at the bottom of the curve, where attraction and repulsion balance. That distance is the bond length. The depth of the dip is the energy released when the bond forms, and the energy you must supply to break it again: the bond enthalpy.

The basis of every bond: a bonded arrangement has lower energy than the separate atoms, so bonding is favoured.

2 · Why bonds form

Bonding uses only the outer electrons.

Only the electrons in the outermost shell, the valence electrons, take part in bonding. For main-group elements, the number of valence electrons equals the last digit of the group number: carbon (group 14) has 4, oxygen (group 16) has 6, chlorine (group 17) has 7.

The noble gases are unreactive because their outer shell is full, usually with eight electrons. This gives a useful shorthand, the octet rule: atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons until each has eight in its outer shell. Hydrogen is the exception: it is complete with two.

The octet rule is a guide, not a law. You will meet molecules that break it in section 3.

2 · Why bonds form

Two main routes: transfer or share.

Ionic

usually metal + non-metal
One atom transfers electrons to the other. Sodium gives its one valence electron to chlorine, forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions held together by electrostatic attraction in a crystal lattice. Example: sodium chloride, the solute in 0.9% saline infusion.

Covalent

usually non-metal + non-metal
Atoms share pairs of electrons. Each shared pair is one covalent bond, and both atoms count it towards their octet. Examples: water, ammonia, and almost every drug molecule.

Polar covalent

sharing, but unequal
The pair is shared, but one atom pulls it harder. That atom becomes slightly negative (δ−). Most real bonds lie somewhere between pure covalent and ionic; section 6 shows how to decide where.

A third type, metallic bonding, holds metals together: metal ions sit in a "sea" of shared, mobile electrons. It is outside the scope of this explainer.

QUESTION 1 OF 3
Why does a covalent bond form between two atoms?
Bonding is favoured because it lowers the energy. The energy released is the bond enthalpy, and the same amount is needed to break the bond.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
On the energy curve, what does the distance at the lowest point represent?
At the minimum, attraction and repulsion balance. That distance is the bond length: 74 pm for H–H. Repulsion is strongest further left, where the curve rises steeply.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
Magnesium (group 2) reacts with oxygen (group 16). What is most likely to happen to the electrons?
Magnesium loses its 2 valence electrons and oxygen gains them, giving Mg²⁺ and O²⁻, each with a full octet. A metal with a non-metal usually means transfer, an ionic bond.
3 · Lewis theory

Lewis: bonds are shared electron pairs.

In 1916, Gilbert N. Lewis proposed that a covalent bond is a pair of electrons shared between two atoms. (Lewis, 1916) A Lewis structure shows every valence electron in a molecule:

  • —a bonding pair is drawn as a line. Two lines make a double bond (2 pairs), three a triple bond (3 pairs).
  • ··a lone pair belongs to one atom only and is drawn as two dots.

Lone pairs matter more than they look. They take up space around an atom and decide the shape of a molecule, as you will see in VSEPR.

3 · Lewis theory · method

Five steps, worked for ammonia (NH₃).

  • 1Skeleton. Put the least electronegative atom in the centre. Hydrogen is never central. Here: N in the middle, three H around it.
  • 2Count all valence electrons. N has 5, each H has 1: 5 + 3 × 1 = 8 electrons. (For an anion add one per negative charge; for a cation subtract.)
  • 3Join each outer atom with a single bond. Three N–H bonds use 3 × 2 = 6 electrons. 2 are left.
  • 4Complete the outer atoms' octets, then put leftovers on the central atom. H is full with 2. The 2 leftover electrons go on N as one lone pair.
  • 5Check the central atom. N now has 3 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair = 8. If it had fewer than 8, you would turn lone pairs on outer atoms into double or triple bonds.
Quick check: the electrons in your final drawing must add up to the count from step 2. If they do not, something is missing or doubled.

How many lone pairs sit on the oxygen atom in water?
8 valence electrons: two O–H bonds use 4, and the remaining 4 sit on O as 2 lone pairs. Those two lone pairs bend the molecule.

3 · Lewis theory · refinements

When more than one drawing works.

Sometimes you can draw several valid Lewis structures for one molecule. Sulfur dioxide, SO₂ (18 valence electrons), is an example: the double bond can go to either oxygen.

O=S–Odouble bond on the left
O–S=Odouble bond on the right

Neither drawing alone is right. Measurements show the two S–O bonds are identical in length. The real molecule is a blend of both drawings, called a resonance hybrid. Resonance structures are linked by a double-headed arrow (↔).

For shape, this does not matter: in both drawings, sulfur has two bonded atoms and one lone pair.

Formal charge = valence electrons − lone-pair electrons − ½ × bonding electrons. Work it out for every atom. The best structure has formal charges as close to zero as possible, with any negative charge on the more electronegative atom.

In O=C=O every atom has a formal charge of 0 (for O: 6 − 4 − ½ × 4 = 0). In the alternative ⁻O–C≡O⁺, the single-bonded O is −1 and the triple-bonded O is +1, so O=C=O is preferred.

The benzene ring found in paracetamol, aspirin and many other drugs is the classic resonance hybrid: its six C–C bonds are all the same length, between a single and a double bond. Its π electrons are spread over the whole ring, which is one reason aromatic drugs absorb ultraviolet light and can be measured by UV spectrophotometry.

4 · Valence bond theory

Where is the shared pair? In the overlap.

Lewis structures say that electrons are shared, but not where they are or why sharing lowers the energy. Valence bond (VB) theory, developed by Heitler, London and Pauling, answers this with atomic orbitals.

  • 1A covalent bond forms when a half-filled orbital on one atom overlaps a half-filled orbital on another.
  • 2The two electrons, with opposite spins, are shared in the overlap region between the nuclei.
  • 3More overlap generally means a stronger bond. Both nuclei attract the shared pair, which is what lowers the energy.
4 · Valence bond theory

Two ways to overlap: σ and π.

A sigma (σ) bond comes from head-on overlap along the line joining the nuclei. It can form from s–s, s–p or p–p (end-on) overlap. Every single bond is a σ bond.

A pi (π) bond comes from side-on overlap of two parallel p orbitals, above and below that line. A π bond only forms alongside a σ bond.

BondMade ofExample
Single1 σC–H in methane
Double1 σ + 1 πC=O in carbon dioxide
Triple1 σ + 2 πN≡N in nitrogen gas
4 · Valence bond theory

Methane breaks the simple picture. Hybrids fix it.

Carbon's ground state has only two half-filled orbitals, so simple overlap predicts two bonds at 90°. Yet methane, CH₄, has four identical C–H bonds pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart.

Pauling's answer was hybridisation: the 2s and the three 2p orbitals mix to form four new, equal orbitals called sp³ hybrids, each with one electron. Each overlaps a hydrogen 1s orbital to make a σ bond.

Rule: the number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of orbitals mixed, and equals the number of electron domains around the atom (bonds plus lone pairs). Lone pairs sit in hybrid orbitals too.
4 · Valence bond theory · tap to compare

Three hybrid sets you must know.

Count the electron domains around the atom (each bonded atom and each lone pair counts once), then read off the hybridisation. Tap all three cards.

For expanded octets, textbooks describe d orbitals joining the mix. sp³d: 5 hybrids, trigonal bipyramidal, e.g. P in PCl₅. sp³d²: 6 hybrids, octahedral, e.g. S in SF₆. Modern calculations show the d orbitals contribute little, but these labels remain a convenient way to match hybridisation to shape.

QUESTION 1 OF 3
The triple bond in nitrogen gas, N≡N, consists of:
Only one head-on (σ) overlap is possible between two atoms. The second and third bonds come from side-on overlap of p orbitals, so they are π bonds.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
What is the hybridisation of carbon in CO₂ (O=C=O)?
Carbon has two electron domains (two double bonds, each counted once) and no lone pairs, so it is sp. Its two unhybridised p orbitals form the two π bonds.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
What is the hybridisation of oxygen in water?
Oxygen has 2 bonding pairs and 2 lone pairs: 4 domains, so sp³. Counting only the bonded atoms (and answering sp) is the most common mistake; lone pairs count too.
5 · VSEPR theory

Electron pairs push apart as far as they can.

Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory, developed by Gillespie and Nyholm, predicts shape from one idea: the electron domains around a central atom repel each other, so they arrange themselves as far apart as possible. (Gillespie & Nyholm, 1957)

An electron domain is any region of electrons around the central atom: a lone pair, or a bond to another atom. A double or triple bond counts as one domain, because all its electrons point the same way.

Chemists write the count as AXₘEₙ: A is the central atom, X a bonded atom, E a lone pair on A.

  • 1Draw the Lewis structure.
  • 2Count X (bonded atoms) and E (lone pairs) on the central atom. Domains = X + E.
  • 3Domains give the electron-domain geometry, how all the pairs are arranged.
  • 4The molecular shape is named from the atoms only, ignoring where the lone pairs are.

5 · VSEPR theory

Lone pairs take more room and squeeze the bonds.

A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it spreads out closer to the central atom and repels more strongly than a bonding pair. The order of repulsion is:

lone–lone > lone–bond > bond–bond

So the same tetrahedral arrangement of four domains gives three different molecular shapes: tetrahedral (CH₄), trigonal pyramidal (NH₃) and bent (H₂O), with the bond angle shrinking each time a lone pair replaces a bond.

Common mistake: calling ammonia "trigonal planar" because it has three hydrogens. The lone pair still occupies a corner, pushing the three N–H bonds down into a pyramid.

QUESTION 1 OF 4
What is the molecular shape of ammonia, NH₃?
AX₃E: four domains (tetrahedral electron geometry), but one is a lone pair, so the shape is trigonal pyramidal. "Tetrahedral" names the electron geometry, not the shape; "trigonal planar" ignores the lone pair.
QUESTION 2 OF 4
Carbon dioxide has two C=O double bonds and no lone pairs on carbon. How many electron domains does carbon have?
Each double bond counts as one domain, so CO₂ is AX₂: linear, 180°. Counting 4 (one per bond line) is the classic mistake.
QUESTION 3 OF 4
Sulfur dioxide is AX₂E. What is its shape?
Three domains give a trigonal planar arrangement, but one is a lone pair. The two O atoms and S form a bent shape, with an angle just under 120°.
QUESTION 4 OF 4
Sulfur hexafluoride, SF₆, has six S–F bonds and no lone pairs on S. What are its shape and bond angle?
AX₆ is octahedral, with 90° between neighbouring bonds. A flat hexagon would put the bonds only 60° apart, far closer than they need to be.
6 · Electronegativity

Who pulls the shared pair harder?

Electronegativity (EN) is the ability of an atom in a bond to attract the shared electrons towards itself. Linus Pauling set up the scale in 1932; fluorine, the most electronegative element, sits at the top with 4.0. (Pauling, 1932)

6 · Bond type

Subtract, then read the scale.

The bond type depends on the difference in electronegativity, ΔEN = larger EN − smaller EN. ΔEN is never negative.

  • 1ΔEN below 0.5: electrons shared almost equally, a non-polar covalent bond.
  • 2ΔEN from 0.5 up to 2.0: shared unequally, a polar covalent bond.
  • 3ΔEN 2.0 or more: electrons effectively transferred, an ionic bond (Chang's rule of thumb).
Rules of thumb, not laws. The boundaries are arbitrary; a compound's properties are the final test. (LibreTexts) B–F scores exactly 2.0, yet BF₃ is a covalent gas. Other books, and the Periodic Trends page on Kompas, use 0.4 and 1.7 instead; follow Chang for your exams.

6 · In pharmacy

Ionic or covalent changes how a drug behaves.

Most drug molecules are covalent, built from C, H, N and O. Many contain a basic nitrogen with a lone pair, shaped like the nitrogen in ammonia. That lone pair can accept a proton from an acid:

Drug basecovalent, neutral R₃N:
+ HCl
Hydrochloride saltionic: R₃NH⁺ Cl⁻

The salt is made of ions, so it usually dissolves far better in water. This is why so many products are supplied as a salt, for example lidocaine hydrochloride injection. The neutral covalent form, in turn, crosses lipid cell membranes more easily. The same drug, bonded two ways, behaves two ways.

Sodium chloride, the classic ionic solid (ΔEN 2.1), is the solute in 0.9% saline. Section 8 explains why ions dissolve so readily in water.

7 · Polarity and dipole moment

Polar bonds do not always make a polar molecule.

A polar bond has a positive end and a negative end: a dipole. Its size is the dipole moment, μ = Q × r (charge × distance), measured in debye (D).

Treat each bond dipole as an arrow pointing to the more electronegative atom, then add the arrows like forces. The molecule is polar only if they do not cancel. So the answer depends on the shape from VSEPR.

CO₂ has two polar C=O bonds, but it is linear: the arrows pull equally in opposite directions, so μ = 0. Water is bent by its two lone pairs, so its arrows add up to 1.85 D.

7 · Polarity · method

Two questions settle most molecules.

Any polar bonds?ΔEN 0.5 or more
Lone pairs on the central atom, or different outer atoms?symmetry broken
Polar moleculeH₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃, SO₂

Answer "no" to the first question and the molecule is non-polar (CH₄). Answer "yes" then "no", meaning a symmetric shape with identical outer atoms, and it is also non-polar (CO₂, BCl₃, CCl₄, SF₆).

In practice: SF₆ in ultrasound. Sulfur hexafluoride is the gas inside SonoVue, an ultrasound contrast agent. Being non-polar, it does not dissolve in water or body fluids, so it stays as tiny gas microbubbles that reflect ultrasound and brighten the image. (EMA)

The test works for the molecules in your course. Some symmetric shapes with lone pairs, such as square planar XeF₄, are non-polar; the lone pairs cancel too.

Quick check: polar or not?

QUESTION 1 OF 3
Carbon dioxide contains two polar C=O bonds. Is the molecule polar?
Polar bonds are necessary but not enough. In a linear O=C=O the two equal dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel, so μ = 0.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
Which of these is a polar molecule?
Dichloromethane has two different outer atoms (H and Cl) on a tetrahedral carbon, so its C–Cl dipoles are not balanced: polar (1.60 D). CCl₄ is symmetric; CH₄ has no polar bonds.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
Why is ammonia polar while boron trichloride is not?
It is the shape. B–Cl (ΔEN 1.0) is actually slightly more polar than N–H (0.9), but BCl₃'s three dipoles cancel at 120°, while NH₃'s pyramidal shape lets them add.
8 · Intermolecular forces

Bonds hold a molecule together. Weaker forces hold molecules to each other.

Intramolecular forces are the covalent bonds within a molecule. Intermolecular forces (IMFs) are the attractions between molecules. They are much weaker, often less than a tenth of a bond's strength.

When water boils, the molecules separate, but every O–H bond stays intact: steam is still H₂O. So boiling point, melting point, volatility and solubility are all controlled by intermolecular forces, and stronger IMFs mean a higher boiling point.

8 · Intermolecular forces · tap all four

Four forces, from weakest to strongest.

For molecules of similar size: London < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding. Ion–dipole forces, which involve full charges, are stronger still. Between molecules of very different size, London forces can outweigh the rest.

8 · Hydrogen bonding

A hydrogen bond needs a donor and an acceptor.

Bonded to N, O or F, hydrogen is stripped of most of its electron density. The tiny, very δ+ hydrogen is strongly attracted to a lone pair on a nearby N, O or F atom.

Trap: formaldehyde contains H and O, but its hydrogens are bonded to carbon. Formaldehyde molecules therefore cannot hydrogen bond to each other; between themselves they have dipole–dipole forces. Water, however, can donate a hydrogen bond to formaldehyde's oxygen, which is why formaldehyde is so soluble in water (as formalin).

8 · Physical properties

Three molecules break the trend.

Going down a group, molecules get bigger, London forces grow, and boiling points rise. Group 14 (CH₄ to SnH₄) follows this neatly; its molecules are non-polar.

In groups 15, 16 and 17 the first member breaks the pattern: NH₃, H₂O and HF boil far higher than their size predicts. They are the only ones with H bonded to N, O or F, so they hydrogen bond.

This answers the opening question: water boils at 100 °C while H₂S, twice as heavy, boils at −60 °C, because water molecules hydrogen bond and H₂S molecules do not.

8 · In pharmacy

Like dissolves like, and volatility can be measured.

Solubility. A substance dissolves well when its intermolecular forces resemble the solvent's. Water, polar and hydrogen bonding, dissolves salts and polar molecules. Non-polar oils dissolve non-polar substances. Choosing a vehicle for a formulation starts here.

Residual solvents. Organic solvents used in manufacture must be removed to tight limits. ICH Q3C groups them by risk: (FDA, 2018)

Gas chromatography. Residual solvents are measured by headspace GC, as in USP <467>. (Shimadzu) Volatility and polarity, both set by intermolecular forces, decide how long each solvent stays on the column: on a non-polar column, compounds come off roughly in order of boiling point.

Quick check: forces between molecules.

QUESTION 1 OF 3
What is the strongest intermolecular force between dichloromethane (CH₂Cl₂) molecules?
CH₂Cl₂ is polar, so dipole–dipole forces act (alongside London forces). Its hydrogens are bonded to carbon, so there is no hydrogen bonding.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
When water boils, what is overcome?
Boiling separates molecules; it does not break them. Steam is still H₂O, so the O–H bonds survive. Only the intermolecular hydrogen bonds (and weaker forces) are overcome.
QUESTION 3 OF 3
Both are non-polar. Why does CCl₄ boil at 76.7 °C but CH₄ at −161.5 °C?
With no dipole–dipole or hydrogen bonding in either, size decides. CCl₄ (74 electrons) is far more polarisable than CH₄ (10), so its London forces are much stronger. Bond strength inside the molecule is irrelevant to boiling.

Five questions, no marks recorded.

QUESTION 1 OF 5 · C1
According to valence bond theory, a covalent bond forms when:
VB theory places the shared pair in the overlap of two half-filled orbitals. Transfer describes ionic bonding, and dipole attraction between molecules is an intermolecular force, not a bond.
QUESTION 2 OF 5 · C2
Phosphorus trichloride, PCl₃, has 26 valence electrons. What is its molecular shape?
Three P–Cl bonds use 6 electrons and the three Cl take 18 as lone pairs, leaving 2 on P: AX₃E, trigonal pyramidal, like NH₃. "Tetrahedral" is the electron-domain geometry; "trigonal planar" forgets the lone pair.
QUESTION 3 OF 5 · C2
Using Chang's values (Al 1.5, Cl 3.0), the Al–Cl bond is:
ΔEN = 3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5, below 2.0: polar covalent. "Metal plus non-metal means ionic" is a first guess that ΔEN overrules here.
QUESTION 4 OF 5 · C3
Which molecule is polar?
Chloroform's mix of H and Cl on a tetrahedral carbon leaves the dipoles unbalanced (1.04 D). CCl₄ and CO₂ have polar bonds arranged symmetrically, so their dipoles cancel.
QUESTION 5 OF 5 · C3
Ammonia (17 g/mol) boils at −33 °C; methane (16 g/mol) at −162 °C. Why the difference?
With almost the same mass, London forces are similar. Ammonia has H on N plus a lone pair, so its molecules hydrogen bond; methane has only London forces. Boiling does not break N–H or C–H bonds.
Take home

Bonds decide shape. Shape decides behaviour.

1
Atoms bond because the bonded arrangement has lower energy. Lewis theory counts the shared and lone pairs; valence bond theory places them in overlapping (often hybrid) orbitals, as σ and π bonds.
2
VSEPR: count bonded atoms and lone pairs on the central atom. Domains set the electron geometry; the atoms alone name the shape. Lone pairs squeeze bond angles.
3
ΔEN classifies a bond (Chang): below 0.5 non-polar, 0.5 up to 2.0 polar covalent, 2.0 or more ionic. These are rules of thumb.
4
A molecule is polar only if its bond dipoles do not cancel. Polarity and hydrogen bonding set the intermolecular forces, which control boiling point, solubility and how solvents behave in GC.
