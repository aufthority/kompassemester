Start here

Same particles, opposite behaviour.

Fluorine and potassium are made of the same three particles: protons, neutrons and electrons. Yet fluorine pulls electrons away from almost anything, and potassium hands its outer electron to almost anything.

The difference comes from one tug-of-war inside every atom. Once you see it, five trends follow: atomic size, ion size, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity. From those come bonds, polarity and reactivity.

Start here

Rows, columns and the two directions.

This explainer uses the main-group elements of the first four rows. The transition metals sit between groups 2 and 13 and are left out to keep the pattern clear.

  • →Across a period (a row): each element has one more proton, and the new electron goes into the same shell.
  • ↓Down a group (a column): each row adds a whole new shell of electrons.

Every trend on the following pages is described in these two directions. Tap any element to see where it sits.

1 · The root cause

The tug-of-war: effective nuclear charge.

The nucleus pulls on the outer electrons. The inner electrons sit in between and block part of that pull; this is called shielding. The pull that is left over is the effective nuclear charge, Zeff.

Two rules carry the rest of this explainer:

  1. 1A bigger Zeff pulls harder.
  2. 2A farther electron feels the pull less.
1 · Effective nuclear charge

Zeff climbs across, and the shell count climbs down.

Across a period, each element adds a proton, but the new electron joins the same shell. Electrons in the same shell shield each other poorly, so Zeff climbs: lithium 1.30, fluorine 5.20.

Down a group, each row adds a new shell. Zeff stays about the same in groups 1 and 2 (sodium and potassium are both 2.20) and rises slowly further right, but the outer electron is now much farther from the nucleus.

Across → Zeff rises, atom shrinksDown ↓ Zeff similar, atom grows

Slater's rules estimate shielding for an s or p electron: each other electron in the same shell shields 0.35, each electron one shell in shields 0.85, and each deeper electron shields 1.00. Then Zeff = Z − shielding.

Chlorine (Z = 17, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵): shielding = (6 × 0.35) + (8 × 0.85) + (2 × 1.00) = 10.90, so Zeff = 6.10. It is a model, not a measurement.

QUESTION 1 OF 3
Going across period 3 from sodium to chlorine, what happens to Zeff?
It rises (2.20 to 6.10). Each step adds a proton, but the new electron joins the same shell, and same-shell electrons shield each other poorly.
QUESTION 2 OF 3
Sodium and potassium both have a Zeff of 2.20, yet potassium holds its outer electron less tightly. Why?
Potassium's outer electron is in the fourth shell, much farther from the nucleus. Same pull, longer distance, weaker hold (rule 2).
QUESTION 3 OF 3
Whose outer electrons feel the strongest pull?
Fluorine: the highest Zeff in period 2 (5.20) and the smallest radius, so both rules point the same way.
2 · Size

Smaller across, bigger down.

Across a period, atoms get smaller: the same shell is pulled in by a stronger Zeff (lithium 128 pm, fluorine 57 pm).

Down a group, atoms get bigger: each row adds a shell, like adding a layer to an onion (fluorine 57, chlorine 102, bromine 120 pm).

1 pm (picometre) = 10⁻¹² m. Values are covalent radii: half the distance between two identical atoms bonded together. Noble gases are hatched because they rarely bond, so their radii are estimates.

Gallium (122 pm) is almost the same size as aluminium (121 pm). The ten 3d electrons added between calcium and gallium shield poorly, so gallium's outer electrons feel a stronger pull than expected. This is the d-block contraction.

2 · Size after a change

Lose electrons and shrink. Gain them and swell.

  • −Sodium (166 pm) becomes Na⁺ (102 pm): it loses its whole outer shell.
  • +Chlorine (102 pm) becomes Cl⁻ (181 pm): one extra electron, the same 17 protons, so more repulsion between electrons and a looser cloud.

So positive ions (cations) are always smaller than their atoms, and negative ions (anions) are always larger.

Compare an ion with its own atom only. Covalent and ionic radii come from different methods, so cross-scale comparisons are rough.

2 · Ionic radius

The same trends, in two families.

The table now shows each element's common ion. Groups 1, 2 and 13 form small cations; groups 16 and 17 form large anions. Hatched elements have no common simple ion.

Down a group, ions grow, for the same reason atoms do: an extra shell each row (Li⁺ 76, Na⁺ 102, K⁺ 138 pm).

Across the cations (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺), size falls sharply, because they have the same electrons but more protons. The next page explains why.

Ionic radii for six-fold coordination (Shannon, 1976).

2 · Ionic radius

Same number of electrons, more protons, smaller ion.

Ions with the same number of electrons are called isoelectronic. All five above have 10 electrons, the same arrangement as neon. The only difference is the number of protons in the nucleus.

More protons pull the same electron cloud in tighter, so the size falls steadily from O²⁻ to Al³⁺.

Small, highly charged cations distort (polarise) large anions, pulling their electron cloud towards themselves. This gives an ionic bond some covalent character (Fajans' rules). Polarising power tracks charge ÷ radius: Na⁺ 1 ÷ 102 = 0.0098, Al³⁺ 3 ÷ 53.5 = 0.056, about 5.7 times stronger.

3 · Holding on

How hard is it to remove an electron?

First ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms:

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻   ΔH is positive

A high value means the atom holds on tightly. Across a period it rises (lithium 520, neon 2081 kJ/mol); down a group it falls (lithium 520, sodium 496, potassium 419 kJ/mol).

Two elements break the pattern. They are outlined in orange on the table; the next page explains them.

3 · Ionisation energy

Two dips that prove subshells exist.

If electrons simply sat in shells, ionisation energy would rise smoothly across a period. It does not. Tap both cards to see why.

The same two dips repeat in period 3: magnesium 738 → aluminium 578, and phosphorus 1012 → sulfur 1000.
3 · Ionisation energy · try it

Removing electrons one by one reveals the shells.

Each electron removed is harder to take than the last, because the ion left behind is more positive. But when the next electron must come from an inner shell, much closer to the nucleus, the energy jumps sharply. Exam questions use this jump to find an element's group.

Element X has the successive ionisation energies in the chart. Which group is it in?
Group 2. The jump comes after the 2nd electron, so X has two outer electrons; the 3rd comes from an inner shell. X is magnesium.
4 · Welcoming one more

How much does an atom welcome an electron?

Electron affinity is the energy change when each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms gains one electron:

X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)

A negative value means energy is released: the atom welcomes the electron. Chlorine releases the most (−349 kJ/mol). In general it becomes more negative across a period and less negative down a group, but with more exceptions than any other trend.

Darker cells release more energy. Some textbooks drop the minus sign (writing 349); it is the same fact.

4 · Electron affinity

Three exceptions worth knowing.

Tap all three cards to continue.

The second electron affinity is always positive: adding an electron to an ion that is already negative costs energy (O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻, about +800 kJ/mol). O²⁻ still exists in compounds because the lattice energy released when the solid forms pays that cost. Section 7 does the same accounting for salt.

5 · Pull inside a bond

Who pulls harder on shared electrons?

Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls the shared electrons in a bond towards itself. The Pauling scale runs from about 0.8 to 4.0: fluorine is highest (3.98), potassium lowest here (0.82).

It rises across a period and falls down a group, for the same two reasons as before.

Across → rises to F 3.98Down ↓ falls: F 3.98 → Br 2.96

Helium, neon and argon have no value: they form no ordinary bonds.

Mulliken defined electronegativity as roughly the average of how hard an atom holds its own electrons (ionisation energy) and how much it welcomes a new one (electron affinity). Pauling built his scale from bond energies instead. The two scales agree closely.

Oxygen's first ionisation energy (1314) is lower than nitrogen's (1402) because:
The paired electrons in one 2p orbital repel each other, so one is easier to remove. Oxygen actually has more protons than nitrogen.
6 · Bonds

From sharing to giving: one scale.

When two atoms bond, how fairly they share the electrons depends on the electronegativity difference, ΔEN.

  • =Equal pull (H–H, Cl–Cl, ΔEN 0): equal sharing, a non-polar covalent bond.
  • ≠Unequal pull (H–Cl, ΔEN 0.96): the shared electrons lean towards chlorine. A polar covalent bond.
  • →Very unequal pull (Na–Cl, ΔEN 2.23): the electron is effectively handed over. An ionic bond.
6 · Bonds

A lopsided bond has partial charges.

In H–Cl, the shared electrons spend more time near chlorine. Chlorine becomes slightly negative, written δ− ("delta minus"), and hydrogen slightly positive, δ+. The bond is a dipole: it has a positive end and a negative end.

Rules of thumb, not laws. Hydrogen fluoride (ΔEN 1.78) sits past the 1.7 line, yet it is still a covalent molecule. Treat the cut-offs as a guide.

Pauling estimated a bond's ionic character from ΔEN: % ionic = 100 × (1 − e−(ΔEN)²/4). H–Cl about 21%, H–F about 55%, Na–Cl about 71%. Even "ionic" sodium chloride keeps some sharing. The calculator on the next page uses this formula.

6 · Molecules

Polar bonds don't always make a polar molecule.

Picture each polar bond as an arrow pointing towards the more electronegative atom. A molecule is polar only if its arrows do not cancel out. Shape decides.

Carbon dioxide has two polar C=O bonds, but the molecule is straight, so the arrows pull equally in opposite directions. Water is bent, so its two arrows add up to a net dipole pointing towards oxygen.

Lone pairs on oxygen bend water (VSEPR). Without them, water would be straight and non-polar.

7 · Reactions, the bridge

A loose hold meets a strong pull.

Atoms on the left of the table hold their outer electrons loosely (low ionisation energy). Atoms on the right, except the noble gases, pull electrons strongly (high electronegativity). Put one of each together and an electron moves.

Every trend so far contributes: sodium is large with a low Zeff, so its outer electron is easy to remove; chlorine is small with a high Zeff, so it pulls hard; the resulting ions attract each other strongly.

The noble gases barely react at all: full shells, very high ionisation energy and no stable negative ion. Nothing to give, and nowhere to put an extra electron.
7 · Reactions

Group 1 and group 17 go opposite ways.

Group 1: more reactive ↓
Li < Na < K. The outer electron is farther away and easier to lose: 520 → 496 → 419 kJ/mol.
Group 17: less reactive ↓
F > Cl > Br. The incoming electron is pulled less strongly: 3.98 → 3.16 → 2.96.
Seen in the lab: chlorine water turns potassium bromide solution orange. Chlorine, the stronger electron-puller, takes the electron from bromide and releases bromine: Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂.

Moving an electron from a gaseous sodium atom to a gaseous chlorine atom actually costs energy: +496 (IE of Na) − 349 (EA of Cl) = +147 kJ/mol. Salt still forms because the ions pack into a crystal and release lattice energy (about −787 kJ/mol). The full accounting is the Born–Haber cycle.

7 · Why it matters in pharmacy

Polarity decides what dissolves in what.

"Like dissolves like." Water is polar, so it dissolves salts and polar molecules. Oils and fats are non-polar, so they dissolve non-polar molecules.

A medicine taken by mouth must dissolve in water in the gut and blood, yet also cross the fatty membranes of cells to be absorbed. So its polarity is a design choice: too polar and it cannot cross membranes; too non-polar and it will not dissolve.

This is also why many drugs with an amine group are supplied as salts, such as hydrochlorides. The ionic salt form dissolves in water far better than the neutral drug.

A drug molecule is almost entirely non-polar. What would you expect?
Non-polar molecules mix poorly with water but pass easily through fatty membranes. Poor water solubility is one of the commonest hurdles in drug formulation.

Take home

One tug-of-war, every trend.

1
Zeff is the nuclear pull left after shielding. A bigger Zeff pulls harder; a farther electron feels it less.
2
Across a period atoms shrink and hold electrons harder; down a group they grow and hold them more loosely.
3
ΔEN places a bond on one scale from non-polar to ionic. Shape decides whether polar bonds make a polar molecule.
4
Polarity decides solubility, which is why a drug's polarity is a design choice in pharmacy.
