Every result rests on a standard.

In your chlorpheniramine STP, every result is worked out against a working standard (WS): its weight (Std Wt) and its potency (P).

If the standard was stored badly, weighed carelessly or recorded wrongly, every result is wrong, and nobody may notice. This chapter covers how labs prevent that.

Reference standards

A substance you can trust completely.

The USP describes reference standards as highly characterised substances used to confirm the identity, strength, quality and purity of medicines.

In short: a sample of the drug whose identity and content are known and documented, so you can compare your sample with it.

Example: to prove a Panadol tablet contains paracetamol, run it by HPLC beside a paracetamol reference standard. Same retention time: same compound.
Reference standards · tap each card

Five jobs in the QC lab.

Reference standards

The official one, and the everyday one.

Primary
Widely accepted as right for its purpose. Its assigned content is used without comparing it with anything else. Example: a pharmacopoeial standard (CRS).
Secondary
Its content is assigned by comparison with a primary standard. Also called a working standard (WS). Used for routine tests.

Why both? Primary standards are expensive and scarce. Labs buy a little, then qualify a larger batch of their own working standard against it.

Source: WHO TRS 943 Annex 3 (glossary); PIC/S GMP Guide 6.20.

Reference standards · tap each card

Who makes them, and how far you trust them.

GMP: if a pharmacopoeial standard exists, use it as the primary standard unless there is a good reason not to. Secondary standards are allowed once their traceability to the primary is shown and documented. (PIC/S GMP 6.20)
Reference standards

Where your “P” comes from.

Primary CRScontent accepted as stated
Candidate batchpure drug substance
Assay against the CRSseveral replicates
Working standardpotency assigned, e.g. 99.6%
Your resultuses that P

The assigned potency is the P in your STP formula. The working standard is re-tested on a schedule. If the primary batch is replaced, the working standard must be recalibrated against the new one. (WHO TRS 943, B.6)

Filling, packaging and storage

Seal out moisture, light and air.

Containers must protect the standard from moisture, light and oxygen. Sealed glass ampoules keep it most stable. (WHO TRS 943, A.6.1)

Fill each container with only what a test needs, ideally single-use. Some standards are filled under inert gas or in controlled humidity, in a glovebox.

Every packaging step is defined and controlled, and the person filling is protected from the substance’s hazards.

Filling, packaging and storage · tap each card

What the label and certificate tell you.

Pharmacopoeial standards usually carry no expiry date in the usual sense; the issuing body tells you which batch is current. (WHO TRS 943, A.6.4–A.6.6)

Filling, packaging and storage

Follow the label, not a habit.

  1. 1Original, tightly closed container. Moisture undoes the benefit of cold storage.
  2. 2The temperature on the label. About 5 °C suits most standards; a few need a freezer, some room temperature. Freezing can harm standards that contain water.
  3. 3Dry and dark. A desiccator for hygroscopic standards; amber glass or a closed box against light.
  4. 4Warm up before opening. Let a cold vial reach room temperature first, or water condenses inside.

Source: WHO TRS 943, A.6.2. Lecture-note correction: “below 4 °C” is not a rule for every standard; always use the stated storage condition.

Good laboratory practice

A quality system for safety studies.

Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) is a quality system for how non-clinical health and environmental safety studies are planned, performed, monitored, recorded, archived and reported. (OECD GLP Principles)

In Malaysia, NPRA and the Department of Standards Malaysia are the GLP Compliance Monitoring Authorities. Malaysia has adhered to the OECD Mutual Acceptance of Data (MAD) system for GLP since 2013, so its GLP study data are accepted abroad. (NPRA)

Its aims: premises fit for the work, staff who are trained and capable, and data of high quality, because testing, recording, reporting and archiving follow a sound approach.

Same habits, three rulebooks.

Malaysia has been a PIC/S member since 2002, and NPRA uses the PIC/S GMP Guide to inspect manufacturers. (NPRA)

In this chapter, “good laboratory practice” means the shared habits all three demand: trained people, suitable premises, controlled equipment, and trustworthy records.

Ten areas a GLP facility controls.

People

Organisation and personnel · quality assurance programme

Place and things

Facilities · apparatus, materials and reagents · test systems · test and reference items

Paper

SOPs · performing the study · reporting results · storing records and materials

Source: OECD Principles of GLP (1997). Note “reference items”: reference standards are part of GLP too.

Good laboratory practice

Someone who checks, and is not checked by the work.

Quality Assurance (QA) assures management that GLP and GMP are being followed through the whole process.

Independent
QA is separate from the people doing the work, so it can judge them fairly.
Witness and enforcer
QA inspects, audits and reports. It does not run the tests itself.

Goal: medicines that reach patients safely and work as intended. Likewise, GMP requires Quality Control to be independent from Production. (PIC/S GMP Ch. 6)

Personnel, premises and equipment

People are the biggest source of contamination.

Wash hands often. Tie long hair back under a hairnet. No jewellery. No eating, drinking or smoking where work is done. (PIC/S GMP Ch. 2)

Protective clothing protects the analyst and the product. No exposed street clothes in the lab, and lab clothing is not worn outside it.

Management must enforce these rules for everyone who enters, visitors included.

Personnel, premises and equipment · tap each card

A lab built for its work.

Source: PIC/S GMP Ch. 3 (Quality Control areas); lecture notes.

Personnel, premises and equipment

Calibrated, maintained, and labelled.

  1. 1Qualified before use: it works as intended where it is installed.
  2. 2Calibrated and checked at defined intervals, set by a written procedure. The interval depends on the instrument and how it is used.
  3. 3Status labelled: calibration due date; faulty equipment clearly marked “out of use”.
  4. 4Instructions and logbook at each instrument: use, cleaning, maintenance, faults.

Source: PIC/S GMP Ch. 3. Lecture-note correction: “twice a year” is one possible schedule, not a fixed rule. A balance, for example, also gets a quick check every day it is used.

Documentation

Not written down, not done.

An inspector cannot see your work, only your records. So every step must leave a trace.

Instructions

SOPs and test methods (your STP): what to do.

Records

Worksheets, notebooks, logbooks, printouts: what was done, by whom, when.

Raw data

The first record of an observation, such as a balance printout. Kept and available. (GMP 6.10)
Documentation · tap each card

Data you can trust: ALCOA+.

Source: MHRA GxP Data Integrity Guidance (2018); WHO and PIC/S use the same terms.

Documentation

One line, still readable.

Draw one line through the wrong entry so it can still be read. Write the correct value beside it, with your initials, the date and, where needed, the reason. (PIC/S GMP Ch. 4)

Never use correction fluid, scribble over, or write a new number on top of the old one. Hiding the original destroys the record.

Laboratory safety

Hazard is the danger; risk is the chance.

Hazard
What can cause harm. Chloroform is toxic if inhaled: that never changes.
Risk
How likely harm is, and how bad. Chloroform in a closed bottle in a fume hood: low risk.

Safety work lowers risk. In Malaysia, the Occupational Safety and Health Act 1994 sets the duty. The USECHH Regulations 2000 control exposure to chemicals hazardous to health, and the CLASS Regulations 2013 bring in GHS labels and safety data sheets. (DOSH)

Laboratory safety

Sixteen sections, four you use most.

Every hazardous chemical comes with a safety data sheet (SDS) in a fixed 16-section order, in Bahasa Malaysia and English. (CLASS Regulations 2013)

Laboratory safety

Remove the danger first; PPE comes last.

Eliminatedo you need it?
Substitutea safer solvent
Engineeringfume hood
AdministrativeSOPs, training
PPEgloves, goggles, coat

Keep incompatible chemicals apart (acids from bases, oxidisers from flammables). Flammables in a flammables cabinet. Keep stock small, labelled and dated.

Warn others and keep them away. Ventilate. Put on the PPE from SDS Section 8 and use the spill kit as Section 6 says. Report it. Large or toxic spills: evacuate and call the emergency team.

Segregate: halogenated solvents (chloroform) apart from other solvents, in labelled, closed containers. Nothing hazardous down the sink.

Practical technique · watch

Weigh by difference.

  1. 1Check the balance is level, clean and has passed its daily check.
  2. 2Close the draught shield; wait for a stable reading.
  3. 3Weigh the vial, tip powder into the flask, weigh the vial again. Mass transferred = before − after.

Weighing by difference counts only what actually went into the flask, even if a few grains stick to the vial.

Practical technique · try it

Too little powder, too big an error.

Every balance wobbles a little. Weigh one test weight 10 times; the spread is the standard deviation (s). USP <41>: 2 × s must be no more than 0.10% of what you weigh, so the minimum weight = 2000 × s.

If s is smaller than 0.41 × the balance’s readability (d), use 0.41d instead. (USP <41>)

Practical technique

Bottom of the curve, at eye level.

Use Class A glassware for standards. A 100 ml Class A flask holds 100 ml within ±0.10 ml. (ISO 1042)

Make up to the mark so the bottom of the meniscus sits on the line, with your eye level with it. Looking from above or below gives the wrong volume.

Pipettes: rinse with the solution first, let it drain freely, and touch the tip to the wall. Do not blow out the last drop unless the pipette says so.

Practical technique

From powder to labelled solution.

Weighby difference
Dissolvepart of the solvent
Make to volumemeniscus on the mark
Mixinvert 10–20 times
Label and recordname, conc., date, initials

Concentration of drug = weight × (P ÷ 100) ÷ volume. This is the stock in your STP before the 4 → 100 ml dilution.

Take home

Right standard, right lab, right record.

1
A reference standard is a fully characterised comparison sample. Primary (e.g. CRS) is accepted as is; secondary (WS) is qualified against it and gives you P.
2
Protect standards from moisture, light and oxygen. Store as labelled, warm before opening, never return material, log every use.
3
OECD GLP covers safety studies; QC labs follow GMP Chapter 6 and often ISO/IEC 17025. All need trained people, fit premises, calibrated equipment and ALCOA+ records.
4
Read the SDS first; contain hazards before relying on PPE. Weigh above the minimum weight, by difference; read the meniscus at eye level.
