From the oil well to the pharmacy shelf.

The gas in a kitchen cylinder, the petrol in a car, the white soft paraffin in an ointment and the plastic of a blister pack all start as the same thing: hydrocarbons from crude oil.

This lab covers how hydrocarbons are built, how to name and draw them by the IUPAC rules, how alkanes react, and how industry separates and converts crude oil into useful products.

Written for Pharmaceutical Chemistry II, Diploma in Pharmacy. Use Next to move through the pages, or Contents to jump around.

Introduction

Only carbon and hydrogen, arranged many ways.

A hydrocarbon is a compound made of carbon and hydrogen atoms only. Carbon forms four bonds, so carbon atoms can link into chains, branches and rings. Hydrocarbons are sorted by the bonds they contain:

Saturated

Single C–C bonds only
  • Alkanes CnH2n+2
  • Cycloalkanes CnH2n

Unsaturated

At least one C=C or C≡C
  • Alkenes CnH2n
  • Cycloalkenes CnH2n−2
  • Alkynes CnH2n−2

Aromatic

A ring with delocalised electrons
  • Benzene C6H6 and its relatives

"Saturated" means the carbons hold the most hydrogen possible. Everything that is not aromatic is called aliphatic.

General formulas are for compounds with one ring or one multiple bond.

Key concept

Why a double bond reacts easily.

A σ (sigma) bond sits directly between the two carbon nuclei. It is strong and hard to reach. Every single bond is a σ bond.

A π (pi) bond forms from side-on overlap of p orbitals, so its electrons sit above and below the bond, exposed to attack. It is weaker than a σ bond.

Alkanes have only strong σ bonds, so they react only under harsh conditions (heat, UV light, a flame). Alkenes and alkynes have exposed π electrons, so reagents add across the double or triple bond easily. You will see both on the reaction pages.

Alkanes · physical properties

Longer chains stick together more.

Alkanes are non-polar: carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities. Their molecules attract each other only by weak London dispersion forces, which grow with the size and surface area of the molecule.

So boiling point climbs with every carbon added. At 0 °C, C1–C4 are gases, C5–C13 are liquids, and C14 upwards are solids.

Liquid: 30 °C is above its melting point (28.2 °C) but far below its boiling point (316 °C). "Phase at room temperature" depends on whose room.
Alkanes · isomerism

Same formula, different molecules.

Structural (constitutional) isomers have the same molecular formula but their atoms are joined differently. Butane, C4H10, has two: a straight chain and a branched chain.

The number of possible isomers explodes as chains grow: C10H22 has 75 and C18H38 has 60,523. A molecular formula alone cannot tell you which compound you have. That is why we need structures and systematic names.

Alkanes · isomerism

More branches, lower boiling point.

The three isomers of C5H12 have the same mass, yet boil at very different temperatures. A straight chain is long and can lie alongside its neighbours over a large surface. A branched molecule is more compact, like a ball, so it touches its neighbours less and the dispersion forces are weaker.

The same happens with C4H10: butane boils at −0.5 °C, branched 2-methylpropane at −11.7 °C.

Isomers can behave differently even with the same formula. In drugs, a change in shape can change how a molecule fits its target or how it is metabolised, which is why medicines are identified by full structure, not formula.

Alkanes · real world

Non-polar, water-repelling, and very useful.

Alkanes do not dissolve in water and are less dense than it, so they float. They are also chemically quiet. Both properties make purified alkane mixtures, called paraffins, common pharmaceutical materials. Tap each card.

Cycloalkanes

Join the ends: a ring.

When the two ends of an alkane chain bond to each other, you get a cycloalkane. Closing the ring removes two hydrogens, so the formula becomes CnH2n, the same as an alkene. Name it by adding cyclo- to the alkane name. Tap each ring.

In skeletal drawings, each corner is a carbon and each carbon carries enough hydrogens to make four bonds.

Cycloalkanes · real world

Many medicines contain rings.

We draw cyclohexane as a flat hexagon, but the real molecule is a puckered chair, which keeps every C–C–C angle near 109.5°.

Saturated rings form the framework of many drug molecules. The steroid skeleton, four fused rings, is shared by cholesterol, hydrocortisone, dexamethasone and the sex hormones. The ring core holds the functional groups in a fixed 3D arrangement that the body's receptors recognise.

Alkenes

One double bond, two fewer hydrogens.

An alkene contains at least one carbon–carbon double bond, C=C. With one double bond, the formula is CnH2n: two hydrogens fewer than the matching alkane.

The two double-bonded carbons and the four atoms attached to them all lie flat, at 120°. The simplest alkene, ethene (ethylene), is the most produced organic chemical in the world: it is the raw material for polyethylene.

Names end in -ene: ethene, propene, butene, and so on.

Alkenes · geometric isomerism

The double bond cannot twist.

Rotating about a C=C would break the π bond, so the groups on each carbon are locked in place. When each carbon carries two different groups, two geometric isomers exist: cis (same side) and trans (opposite sides). They are different compounds: cis-but-2-ene boils at about 4 °C, trans-but-2-ene at about 1 °C.

Tretinoin (all-trans retinoic acid) and isotretinoin (13-cis retinoic acid) differ only in the geometry of one double bond. They are separate medicines with different uses and doses, used for acne by different routes.

Which alkene has NO cis/trans isomers?
In but-1-ene, CH₂=CH–CH₂CH₃, the first carbon carries two identical H atoms, so swapping sides gives the same molecule.
Cycloalkenes

A ring with a double bond.

A cycloalkene has a double bond inside a ring. The ring removes two hydrogens and the double bond removes two more, so the formula is CnH2n−2.

When naming, the two double-bond carbons are always numbered 1 and 2, so the simplest ones need no number: cyclopentene, cyclohexene.

Cycloalkene rings appear in plant chemistry: limonene, the fragrant oil in citrus peel used to flavour and scent medicines, is built on a cyclohexene ring.

Beyond alkenes

Two more families to recognise.

Alkynes, C≡C
One triple bond, formula CnH2n−2, linear at 180°. Names end in -yne. Ethyne (acetylene) burns very hot in welding torches. With two π bonds, an alkyne adds two molecules of a reagent, e.g. HC≡CH + 2Br2 → CHBr2CHBr2.
Aromatics: benzene
A flat six-carbon ring with six π electrons spread evenly around it (delocalised). This makes benzene unusually stable, by about 150 kJ/mol, so it resists addition and undergoes substitution instead. Many drugs, e.g. paracetamol and aspirin, contain a benzene ring.
Benzene itself is a known carcinogen. It is an ICH Class 1 solvent, limited to 2 ppm in medicines.

IUPAC naming

Four rules, always in this order.

  1. 1Find the parent chain: the longest continuous chain of carbons, even if it bends round corners. If two chains tie, choose the one with more branches.
  2. 2Number it from the end that gives the branches the lowest numbers (locants). Compare locant sets term by term; the first difference decides.
  3. 3Name the branches (methyl, ethyl…) and list them in alphabetical order. Use di-, tri-, tetra- for repeats, but ignore these prefixes when alphabetising.
  4. 4Punctuate: commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and words. Example: 3-ethyl-2,2-dimethylhexane.

IUPAC naming · alkyl groups

Naming branches, classifying carbons.

Reactions of alkanes

"Paraffin" means little affinity.

The old name for alkanes, paraffins, comes from Latin parum affinis, "little affinity". At room temperature, alkanes ignore acids, bases and most oxidising agents.

The reason: C–C and C–H bonds are strong σ bonds, and they are non-polar, so there is no partly positive or partly negative site for a reagent to attack. Breaking these bonds needs a lot of energy.

Alkanes have two important reactions, both needing energy to start:

Combustion
Burning in oxygen, started by a spark or flame.
Halogenation
Substitution with chlorine or bromine, started by UV light or heat.
This inertness is exactly why paraffins are trusted ointment bases: they will not react with the drug they carry.

With plenty of oxygen, an alkane burns completely to carbon dioxide and water, releasing a lot of heat. That heat is why alkanes are our main fuels. Watch methane burn:

General rule for complete combustion: CnH2n+2 + (3n+1)/2 O2 → n CO2 + (n+1) H2O. When the oxygen number is a fraction, double everything.

Reactions of alkanes · combustion

Too little oxygen: a silent poison.

When oxygen is limited, carbon is only partly oxidised. The products include carbon monoxide (CO) and soot (carbon), as well as water. Step through methane burning in limited oxygen:

CO has no colour or smell. It binds to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen does, so the blood carries less oxygen. Early symptoms (headache, dizziness, nausea) are easily mistaken for flu.

Running a generator, a charcoal stove or a gas heater in a closed room can kill. A yellow, sooty flame instead of a blue one is a sign of incomplete combustion.
Reactions of alkanes · halogenation

A chain reaction, powered by radicals.

In UV light, an alkane swaps a hydrogen for a halogen: CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl. This is a substitution reaction, and it runs through free radicals: atoms with an unpaired electron, shown as a dot. Step through all three stages.

Alkanes vs alkenes

Alkenes react easily.

Alkenes undergo addition: the π bond opens and two new atoms attach, one to each carbon. No UV light is needed.

This gives a simple test. With an alkene, the orange colour of bromine water disappears. With an alkane (in the dark), it stays orange.

Hydrogenation (H2, nickel catalyst) turns liquid vegetable oils into solid fats; hydrogenated vegetable oil and hydrogenated castor oil are used as tablet lubricants. Hydration (steam, H3PO4 catalyst, about 300 °C and 60 atm) turns ethene into ethanol.

Industry · refining

One column, many fractions.

Crude oil is a mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons, mostly alkanes and cycloalkanes. A refinery first heats it to about 400 °C, turning most of it to vapour, and feeds it into the bottom of a tall fractionating column.

The column is hot at the bottom and cool at the top. As vapour rises through trays, each compound condenses when it reaches a tray cooler than its boiling point. Small molecules with low boiling points rise to the top; large molecules condense near the bottom. Each tray collects a fraction: a mixture with a range of boiling points.

Industry · cracking

Cracking: long chains into short ones.

Distillation produces more heavy fractions than people buy, and less petrol and ethene than industry needs. Cracking fixes this: long alkanes are heated, often over a catalyst (a zeolite at about 500 °C), so C–C bonds break. The products are a shorter alkane and an alkene.

Balance it: C10H22 → C8H18 + ?
Carbon: 10 − 8 = 2. Hydrogen: 22 − 18 = 4. The product is ethene, the alkene that industry wants most. You can confirm it is an alkene with bromine water.

Real world · naming meets the petrol station

What does RON95 mean?

Petrol must burn smoothly, not explode early ("knock") in the engine. Its octane rating compares it with two reference alkanes: straight-chain heptane scores 0, and a highly branched octane, "isooctane", scores 100. RON95 petrol resists knocking like a 95 : 5 mixture of isooctane and heptane. Branched alkanes burn more smoothly, which is one reason refineries rearrange chains.

Take home

Structure explains behaviour.

1
Alkanes (CnH2n+2) and cycloalkanes (CnH2n) are saturated; alkenes (CnH2n) and cycloalkenes (CnH2n−2) contain a reactive C=C.
2
Boiling point rises with chain length and falls with branching, because both change the dispersion forces between molecules.
3
IUPAC names: longest chain, lowest locants at the first point of difference, branches in alphabetical order.
4
Alkanes burn (completely to CO2 and water, or incompletely to toxic CO) and undergo free-radical substitution in UV light. Refineries separate crude oil by boiling point and crack heavy fractions into fuels and alkenes.
