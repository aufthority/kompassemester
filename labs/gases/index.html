Gases and pressure · watch, then tap each card

Four things every gas does.

Only 11 elements are gases at 25 °C and 1 atm, for example H₂, N₂, O₂ and the noble gases. Many small molecules are gases too: CO₂, N₂O, NH₃. (Chang 5.1)

Gases and pressure

Pressure = force ÷ area.

Gas molecules hit every surface around them. The force of these hits on each unit of area is the pressure. The SI unit is the pascal: 1 Pa = 1 N/m².

1 atm
One standard atmosphere: air pressure at sea level. Used in this chapter, as in Chang.
760 mmHg
= 1 atm. The height of a mercury column the air can hold up. 1 mmHg = 1 torr.
101 325 Pa
= 1 atm = 101.325 kPa.
1.013 bar
= 1 atm. Cylinder labels often use bar.
Blood pressure is still written in mmHg: 120/80 mmHg. To change mmHg to atm, divide by 760.
Gases and pressure · open each one

Two ways to measure it.

Measures air pressure. At sea level the air holds up a mercury column 760 mm high: 1 atm. (Chang 5.2)

A manometer compares a gas with another pressure. Most cylinder and tyre gauges show pressure above the air around them (gauge pressure). An “empty” cylinder still holds gas at 1 atm.

The gas laws need the true (absolute) pressure: gauge reading + about 1 atm.
The gas laws · watch

Squeeze it: pressure goes up.

Boyle’s law: at constant temperature and amount, the volume of a gas is inversely proportional to its pressure.

P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ (T and n constant)

Halve the volume and the pressure doubles. This is why a 4.7 L cylinder at 135 atm can give about 135 × 4.7 ≈ 630 L of oxygen at 1 atm.

Source: Chang 5.3.

The gas laws

Heat it: it expands.

Charles’s law: at constant pressure and amount, volume is directly proportional to the absolute temperature.

V₁ ÷ T₁ = V₂ ÷ T₂ (P and n constant)

Temperatures in gas laws are always in kelvin: T (K) = t (°C) + 273. In °C the law does not work: 0 °C is not “no temperature”.

Chang uses 273 for routine sums and 273.15 for precise work. (Chang 5.3)

The gas laws · watch, then try it

Heat a closed can: pressure rises.

In a rigid container the volume cannot change. At constant volume and amount, pressure is proportional to absolute temperature: P₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂ ÷ T₂.

Hence the spray-can label: “do not expose to temperatures exceeding 50 °C”. Usually called Gay-Lussac’s law (Chang: a form of Charles’s law; Silberberg: Amontons’s law).

The gas laws

More gas, more volume.

Avogadro’s law: at constant pressure and temperature, volume is directly proportional to the number of moles: V ∝ n.

Equal volumes of different gases at the same temperature and pressure hold the same number of molecules, whatever the gas.

At STP (standard temperature and pressure: 0 °C and 1 atm), 1 mole of any ideal gas takes up 22.41 L. (Chang 5.4)

The ideal gas equation · try it

Four laws, one equation.

PV = nRT

P in atm, V in L, n in mol, T in K. R = 0.0821 L·atm/(K·mol) (Chang 5.4)

Hold any two of P, V, T or n constant and one of the four laws appears. A gas that obeys PV = nRT exactly is an ideal gas.

The ideal gas equation · try it

STP: a fixed point to compare gases.

Gas volumes change with T and P, so we compare them at standard temperature and pressure (STP): 0 °C (273.15 K) and 1 atm. One mole of an ideal gas at STP:

V = nRT ÷ P = 1 × 0.0821 × 273.15 ÷ 1 = 22.41 L

Try 25 °C: the same mole now fills about 24.5 L. Always check which conditions a volume refers to. (Chang 5.4; Silberberg 5.3)

The ideal gas equation · open each one

Before and after: the combined law.

When the amount of gas stays the same, PV ÷ T is constant:

P₁V₁ ÷ T₁ = P₂V₂ ÷ T₂

40.0 ml at 1.00 atm and 20 °C goes to 37 °C and 1.20 atm.
V₂ = 40.0 × (1.00 ÷ 1.20) × (310 ÷ 293) = 35.3 ml.

T is constant, so Boyle’s law: the 4.7 L cylinder at 60 atm (gauge) gives 60 × 4.7 = 282 L at 1 atm. The last 4.7 L stays inside at 1 atm, which is why the gauge reading is used.

282 L ÷ 4 L/min = about 70 minutes. Not enough for 90 minutes: take a full cylinder.

You will do this with new numbers at Gas Bench station 1.

The ideal gas equation

Weigh a gas, name the gas.

Rearranging PV = nRT, with n = mass ÷ molar mass (ℳ):

d = Pℳ ÷ RT
Density of a gas, in g/L. Heavier molecules, denser gas.
ℳ = dRT ÷ P
Measure the density, get the molar mass, identify the gas.
Medicines and vaccines are often shipped on dry ice. Its CO₂ (ℳ = 44.01) has a density of 1.80 g/L at 25 °C and 1 atm, against 1.18 g/L for air. CO₂ sinks and collects near the floor of closed rooms and vans. Ventilate before entering.

At Gas Bench station 3 you identify an inhaler propellant this way. (Chang 5.4)

The ideal gas equation

How much gas does a reaction make?

An effervescent tablet fizzes because sodium bicarbonate reacts with citric acid:

3NaHCO₃ + C₆H₈O₇ → Na₃C₆H₅O₇ + 3H₂O + 3CO₂

1.00 g NaHCO₃÷ 84.01 g/mol
0.0119 mol NaHCO₃3 : 3 ratio
0.0119 mol CO₂V = nRT ÷ P
0.291 L CO₂at 25 °C, 1 atm

Grams → moles → mole ratio → moles of gas → volume (with PV = nRT, or 22.41 L/mol at STP only).

That is why effervescent tablets are packed in tightly closed, dry tubes: moisture starts the reaction early. (Chang 5.5)

Gas mixtures

Each gas pushes on its own.

Dalton’s law: the total pressure of a mixture is the sum of the pressures each gas would have alone.

PT = P₁ + P₂ + …   and   Pi = Xi PT

X is the mole fraction: moles of that gas ÷ total moles.

Entonox (pain relief in labour and ambulances) is 50% N₂O and 50% O₂. Each gas gives half the total pressure. Air is about 21% O₂: at 1 atm, PO₂ ≈ 0.21 atm. (Chang 5.6)
Gas mixtures

Don’t forget the water vapour.

Hydrogen peroxide breaks down to oxygen: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Collected over water, the tube holds O₂ and water vapour.

PO₂ = Patm − PH₂O

Water vapour pressure depends only on temperature: 23.76 mmHg at 25 °C (Chang Table 5.3). Subtract it before using PV = nRT.

You will check a hydrogen peroxide solution this way at Gas Bench station 4.

Kinetic molecular theory · tap each card

A gas is molecules in motion.

Chang lists four postulates; Silberberg groups them into three. (Chang 5.7)

Kinetic molecular theory · open each one

Why the laws work.

Pressure is molecules hitting the walls: how often and how hard they hit.

Less volume, more molecules in each ml: they hit the walls more often, so pressure rises.

Higher T, faster molecules: they hit harder and more often. In a closed can the pressure rises; with a free piston the gas expands until the pressures balance.

More molecules, more hits. To keep the same pressure, the volume must grow.

Molecules don’t attract or repel (postulate 3), so each gas hits the walls as if it were alone. Their pressures simply add up.

(Chang 5.7)

Kinetic molecular theory · compare

Hotter or lighter: faster.

Molecules in a gas move at many different speeds. Two simple rules:

  • Higher temperature: more energy, so molecules move faster on average.
  • Same temperature, lighter molecules: same average energy, so they move faster than heavy ones.
So light gases spread (diffuse) faster. Heliox (helium with oxygen) flows through a narrowed airway more easily than air, because helium is so light. (Chang 5.7)

Real gases

PV = nRT is a model.

For 1 mol of an ideal gas, PV/RT = 1 at every pressure. Real gases stay close to 1 only at low pressure (about 5 atm or less) and high temperature.

Deviations grow when:

  • Pressure is high: molecules are crowded, so their size and attractions matter.
  • Temperature is low: slow molecules are held back by attractions. Without attractions, gases could never condense to liquids.

Medical cylinders work at about 135 atm: well outside the ideal range. (Chang 5.8)

Real gases

Two corrections to PV = nRT.

(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT

Neighbours pull each molecule back as it hits the wall, so the measured pressure is lower than ideal. The term adds this back. a shows how strongly the molecules attract each other.

Molecules take up some space, so the free volume is smaller than the container. b roughly reflects molecular size.

P is the measured pressure and V the container’s volume. (Chang 5.8)

Real gases · in the pharmacy

Some cylinders hold a liquid.

Gases with strong attractions (large a) turn liquid when squeezed at room temperature. N₂O and CO₂ cylinders hold liquid with gas above it.

O₂, air
Gas only. The gauge falls steadily as gas is used, so pressure shows the contents.
N₂O, CO₂
Liquid + gas. The gauge stays steady until the liquid is gone, so weigh the cylinder to know the contents.
An N₂O cylinder’s gauge reads “full” until only about a quarter of its contents is left. A steady gauge does not mean a full cylinder.

The pressure above a liquid depends only on temperature (vapour pressure), not on how much liquid is left.

1
Boyle P₁V₁ = P₂V₂; Charles V ∝ T; Gay-Lussac P ∝ T; Avogadro V ∝ n. Always use kelvin and absolute pressure.
2
PV = nRT with R = 0.0821 L·atm/(K·mol). STP is 0 °C and 1 atm: 22.41 L/mol. d = Pℳ ÷ RT. Dalton: Pi = XiPT; subtract water vapour.
3
Kinetic theory: far-apart molecules in random motion, no forces, average kinetic energy ∝ T. Pressure is molecules hitting the walls.
